Pilots practise in a simulator. New callers practise on your customers.

It’s Tuesday morning. Your new colleague has spent two days listening in, got a script and a headset, and is now taking their first real call. On the other end is a customer calling for the third time about their invoice. Or a prospect who says they’re not interested after ten seconds.

Your colleague does their best. But they don’t know the answer straight away, put the customer on hold twice and promise to call back. Or they give up at the first objection. The customer hangs up without a solution.

This is how most new callers learn the job: on real customers. Which is strange, when you think about it. A pilot practises an engine failure in a simulator first, not with passengers on board. A doctor practises resuscitation on a mannequin first. But a new caller practises their hardest conversations live, with your customers as practice material.

The first weeks cost more than you think

Finding new people is hard. Contact centre agent is on the list of shortage occupations in Flanders for 2026 (VDAB, 2026). So vacancies are hard to fill.

And the people you find often leave quickly. In Belgium, 1 in 8 permanent contracts is terminated within the first year, and 8.7 percent even within six months. That’s according to figures from more than 31,000 Belgian employers (Acerta, May 2025). So the first months are the most fragile. Anyone who spends those weeks mostly doubting themselves and making mistakes on the phone has little reason to stay.

And the customer notices straight away. Test Aankoop asked around 1,500 Belgians what matters to them in a customer service: an answer to their question, how quickly it gets solved, how much effort it takes to reach the right person, and whether that person knows their job (Test Aankoop, 2024). That’s exactly where a new colleague falls short in the first weeks. For them, that first call is practice. For the customer on the other end, it isn’t.

Why listening in and role-play aren’t enough

Most teams onboard new people with three things. All three are useful, but none of them lets someone really practise.

Listening in. You learn how an experienced colleague does it, but you don’t do it yourself. Like learning to ride a bike by watching the Tour de France.

Role-play with a colleague. Everyone knows it: it feels awkward, the colleague doesn’t play a truly difficult customer, and in a busy week it’s the first thing to go.

A course or training. Good for the basics, but a training day is a single moment. Researchers in Paris combined 29 studies and found a clear difference: what you practise spread out over time sticks much better than what you learn in one go (Latimier, Peyre and Ramus, 2021). A training day on Monday doesn’t help much if the first difficult call only comes two weeks later.

What does work: practising like pilots and doctors

In healthcare, this is well researched. A French review from December 2024 combined 20 studies with more than 3,300 participants in total. People who first practised conversations in a simulation, for example with an actor playing a patient, became more confident and communicated better (Foucault-Fruchard et al., 2024).

It doesn’t work because a simulation is high-tech, but because of two simple things:

  • Repetition in a safe environment. A mistake costs nothing, so you dare to do the difficult conversation five times instead of once.
  • Immediate feedback. After every attempt you hear what could be better, and you try again while it’s still fresh.

Translate that to the phone and you get this: a new colleague first has the conversation with the angry customer, or the prospect who says “not interested”, a few times in a practice setting. Only then do they have it with a real customer.

Four things you can start doing tomorrow

You don’t need a tool for this. You can start tomorrow.

  1. Make a list of the ten hardest conversations. Ask your experienced callers where new colleagues most often get stuck: an angry customer, a request for a discount, “just send me an email”, a customer who speaks French.
  2. Have each of those conversations practised before someone goes live. Not once, but three to five times, until it goes smoothly.
  3. Give feedback the same day, one point at a time. “Your opening was strong. Next time, first ask what the customer has already tried.” Nobody remembers ten comments at once.
  4. Measure one number: the days until someone calls on their own. What you measure, you can improve. If you see that number go down, you know your approach is working.
